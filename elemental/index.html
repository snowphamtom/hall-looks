H

Hydrogen

Atomic number 1 · Bulk

Body store
7,000 g (10% of body mass)
Where it lives
Water (11% of water's mass, but ~4.2 x 10^27 atoms - the most numerous atom in the body); every organic molecule; free H+ defining pH.
Key figures
Blood pH 7.35-7.45 = [H+] 35-45 nmol/L. Intracellular pH ~7.0-7.2. Stomach pH 1.5-3.5.
Molecules
H2O; -CH2-/-CH3; -NH2/-NH3+; -OH; -SH.
Reactions
The mitochondrial proton gradient (~150-180 mV) drives ATP synthase (~3-4 H+ per ATP). Bicarbonate buffer: CO2 + H2O <-> H+ + HCO3-; lungs vent CO2, kidneys regenerate HCO3-.
Deficiency
Acidosis (pH <7.35) depresses enzymes and consciousness; alkalosis (pH >7.45) causes tetany and arrhythmia. Death outside ~6.8-7.8.
Toxicity
Same as deficiency - framed as acid-base disorders.
Sources
Water; all food.

C

Carbon

Atomic number 6 · Bulk

Body store
12,600 g (18% of body mass)
Where it lives
Backbone of all proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, nucleic acids. Fat tissue ~77% carbon; protein ~53%.
Key figures
~12,600 g; ~6.3 x 10^26 atoms. Daily turnover: ~300 g exhaled as CO2 at rest (~200-250 mL/min CO2).
Molecules
Amino acid backbones, fatty acid chains, glucose rings, nucleotide bases, steroid rings (cholesterol), porphyrin rings (heme).
Reactions
Tetravalent - 4 covalent bonds: chains, branches, rings. Glycolysis, Krebs cycle, beta-oxidation are all carbon skeleton rearrangement.
Deficiency
No elemental deficiency state (carbon is never limiting).
Toxicity
Toxicity is molecular (CO, cyanide), not elemental.
Sources
All food - carbohydrates, fats, proteins.
N

Nitrogen

Atomic number 7 · Bulk

Body store
2,100 g (3% of body mass)
Where it lives
All amino acids (~16% of protein mass is N); DNA/RNA bases; urea; neurotransmitters (glutamate, GABA, dopamine, serotonin); nitric oxide (NO).
Key figures
~2,100 g. Daily N loss ~10-15 g (urine urea ~85%, feces, skin, sweat).
Molecules
-NH2, peptide bonds (-CO-NH-), purine/pyrimidine rings, urea CO(NH2)2, NO, creatine.
Reactions
Transamination shuttles -NH2 between molecules; deamination feeds the urea cycle (liver: 2 NH3 + CO2 -> urea). NO synthase: arginine -> NO (vasodilation).
Deficiency
Protein-energy malnutrition - muscle wasting, edema (kwashiorkor), immune collapse.
Toxicity
Hyperammonemia (liver failure) - NH3 crosses the blood-brain barrier, causing encephalopathy.
Sources
Dietary protein (meat, dairy, legumes, grains).
O

Oxygen

Atomic number 8 · Bulk

Body store
45,500 g (65% of body mass)
Where it lives
Body water (~60% of mass; water is 89% oxygen by mass); every organic molecule; blood as oxyhemoglobin; bone mineral (phosphate, hydroxide).
Key figures
~42-43 L body water (intracellular ~28 L, extracellular ~14-15 L). Resting O2 use ~250 mL/min; brain takes ~20% (~50 mL/min). Arterial O2 saturation 95-100%; PaO2 80-100 mmHg.
Molecules
H2O; hydroxyl (-OH), carbonyl (C=O), carboxyl (-COOH); O2 bound to hemoglobin (4 per molecule); oxyanions (phosphate, sulfate, bicarbonate).
Reactions
Glucose + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O + ~30-32 ATP. O2 is the terminal electron acceptor of the electron transport chain. Leaked superoxide is dismutated by SODs, finished by catalase and glutathione peroxidase.
Deficiency
Hypoxia - minutes without O2 irreversibly injure brain (neurons die after ~4-6 min of anoxia).
Toxicity
Hyperoxia - prolonged >60% O2 damages lungs; >1.6 atm O2 causes CNS toxicity (seizures).
Sources
Air (21% O2); water and food (as H2O and biomolecules).
F

Fluorine

Atomic number 9 · Trace

Body store
3-6 g
Where it lives
Teeth (enamel/dentin) and bone - nowhere else in quantity.
Key figures
Adequate intake 4 mg (men) / 3 mg (women). Optimal water ~0.7 mg/L.
Molecules
Fluorapatite Ca10(PO4)6F2 - F- swaps into hydroxyapatite's OH- slot.
Reactions
Fluorapatite resists acid dissolution far better than hydroxyapatite - the entire mechanism of cavity prevention: harder enamel.
Deficiency
Higher caries rate (not a true deficiency disease).
Toxicity
Dental fluorosis (mottling); skeletal fluorosis (chronic very high intake).
Sources
Fluoridated water, tea, fish.

Na

Sodium

Atomic number 11 · Electrolyte

Body store
105 g (0.15% of body mass)
Where it lives
Extracellular fluid (~140 mmol/L); ~30-40% also bound in bone (exchangeable reservoir).
Key figures
Serum 135-145 mmol/L. Daily need <2,300 mg. Plasma osmolality ~285 mOsm/kg.
Molecules
Free Na+ ion.
Reactions
Na+ influx is the action potential upstroke. Drives glucose/amino acid uptake (Na+-coupled transporters). Where Na+ goes, water follows - ADH/aldosterone regulate volume via Na+.
Deficiency
Hyponatremia -> cerebral edema, seizures.
Toxicity
Hypernatremia -> cellular dehydration; chronic excess linked to hypertension.
Sources
Salt (NaCl), processed foods, bread.
Mg

Magnesium

Atomic number 12 · Mineral

Body store
35 g (0.05% of body mass)
Where it lives
60% bone, 39% intracellular, ~1% extracellular - the active fraction.
Key figures
Serum 0.7-1.0 mmol/L. Daily need 400 mg (men) / 310 mg (women). Cofactor for 300+ enzymes.
Molecules
Mg-ATP2- (the true substrate of kinases); Mg2+ in DNA/RNA polymerases, ribosomes.
Reactions
Every ATP reaction actually uses Mg-ATP. Mg2+ plugs the NMDA receptor at rest - ejected during strong signals, enabling learning (LTP). Antagonizes Ca2+ (muscle relaxation).
Deficiency
Hypomagnesemia -> refractory hypokalemia/hypocalcemia, tetany, torsades de pointes.
Toxicity
Hypermagnesemia -> loss of reflexes, respiratory depression, cardiac arrest.
Sources
Nuts, seeds, whole grains, leafy greens, legumes.

P

Phosphorus

Atomic number 15 · Bulk

Body store
700 g (1.0% of body mass)
Where it lives
85% bone/teeth (hydroxyapatite); 15% in ATP/ADP, DNA/RNA, phospholipids, phosphoproteins, buffers.
Key figures
Serum phosphate 0.8-1.5 mmol/L. Daily need 700 mg. Body recycles its own weight in ATP daily.
Molecules
ATP/ADP/AMP; DNA/RNA backbone; phospholipid heads; hydroxyapatite; 2,3-BPG in red cells.
Reactions
Phosphorylation is the universal protein on/off switch (~500 human kinases). ATP -> ADP + Pi releases ~30.5 kJ/mol. With calcium: Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2 mineralizes bone.
Deficiency
Hypophosphatemia -> muscle weakness, rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis (refeeding syndrome).
Toxicity
Hyperphosphatemia (kidney failure) -> vascular calcification.
Sources
Meat, dairy, nuts, whole grains.
S

Sulfur

Atomic number 16 · Electrolyte

Body store
175 g (0.25% of body mass)
Where it lives
Methionine and cysteine (amino acids); glutathione; iron-sulfur clusters; cartilage (chondroitin sulfate); taurine; CoA.
Key figures
~175 g; requirement met by protein intake.
Molecules
-SH thiols; disulfide (-S-S-) bridges; Fe-S clusters; sulfate esters.
Reactions
Disulfides lock protein shapes (insulin, antibodies). Glutathione redox: 2GSH + H2O2 -> GSSG + 2H2O (Se-dependent). Fe-S clusters ferry electrons in respiration.
Deficiency
Manifests as protein deficiency (sulfur amino acids limiting).
Toxicity
H2S gas is toxic (blocks cytochrome c oxidase); sulfite cleared by Mo-dependent sulfite oxidase.
Sources
Protein foods (eggs, meat); garlic/onions (organosulfur compounds).
Cl

Chlorine

Atomic number 17 · Electrolyte

Body store
105 g (0.15% of body mass)
Where it lives
Extracellular fluid (98-107 mmol/L); stomach acid as HCl; red blood cells (chloride shift).
Key figures
Serum 98-107 mmol/L. Daily need ~2,300 mg (with sodium).
Molecules
Cl- ion; HCl (pH 1.5-3.5).
Reactions
Chloride shift ferries CO2 to the lungs (Cl- in, HCO3- out of red cells). GABA-A receptors are Cl- channels - inhibition is chloride flowing in. Gastric HCl sterilizes food, activates pepsin.
Deficiency
Hypochloremia (with metabolic alkalosis, vomiting).
Toxicity
Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis (excess saline).
Sources
Salt; same as sodium.

K

Potassium

Atomic number 19 · Electrolyte

Body store
245 g (0.35% of body mass)
Where it lives
98% intracellular (~140 mmol/L inside vs 3.5-5.0 outside); muscle holds ~75% of body K+.
Key figures
Serum 3.5-5.0 mmol/L. Daily need 3,400 mg (men) / 2,600 mg (women). Resting membrane potential ~-70 to -90 mV.
Molecules
Free K+ ion - no structural role; pure electrochemistry.
Reactions
Na+/K+-ATPase: 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in per ATP (~25-40% of resting energy). K+ efflux repolarizes every action potential.
Deficiency
Hypokalemia -> arrhythmia, muscle weakness, ileus.
Toxicity
Hyperkalemia -> peaked T waves, widened QRS, asystole - the most acutely lethal electrolyte abnormality.
Sources
Fruits, vegetables, legumes, potatoes, bananas.
Ca

Calcium

Atomic number 20 · Bulk

Body store
1,050 g (1.5% of body mass)
Where it lives
99% in bone/teeth as hydroxyapatite Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2; 1% in blood and cells - the signaling fraction.
Key figures
Serum total 2.15-2.55 mmol/L; ionized 1.15-1.33 mmol/L; intracellular resting ~100 nmol/L (10,000:1 gradient). Daily need 1,000 mg. Bone remodels ~10%/year.
Molecules
Hydroxyapatite crystal; calmodulin, troponin C; clotting factors II, VII, IX, X (Ca2+-dependent).
Reactions
Muscle contraction (Ca2+ binds troponin); neurotransmitter release (Ca2+ influx at synapses); clotting cascade (factor IV); second-messenger spikes. PTH withdraws Ca2+ from bone when blood runs low.
Deficiency
Hypocalcemia -> tetany, seizures, prolonged QT; chronic -> osteoporosis.
Toxicity
Hypercalcemia -> kidney stones, arrhythmia, confusion.
Sources
Dairy, leafy greens, fortified foods, small fish with bones.

Mn

Manganese

Atomic number 25 · Trace

Body store
~12 mg
Where it lives
Mitochondria (Mn-SOD), bone, liver, pancreas, pituitary.
Key figures
Blood 4-15 ug/L. Daily adequate intake 2.3 mg (men) / 1.8 mg (women).
Molecules
Mn-superoxide dismutase (mitochondrial); arginase; pyruvate carboxylase.
Reactions

Fe

Iron

Atomic number 26 · Trace

Body store
4-5 g
Where it lives
Hemoglobin 65% (~2.5-3 g), myoglobin 10%, cytochromes/Fe-S ~5%, ferritin stores ~20% (liver, spleen, marrow).
Key figures
Hb 13.5-17.5 g/dL (men), 12.0-15.5 (women). Ferritin 15-200 ug/L. Daily need 8 mg (men) / 18 mg (women); 27 mg pregnancy.
Molecules
Heme b (protoporphyrin IX + Fe2+); Fe-S clusters; ferritin cage (4,500 Fe atoms); transferrin.
Reactions
Fe2+ + O2 <-> Fe2+-O2 (reversible - the whole trick of breathing). Cytochromes cycle Fe2+/Fe3+. Free iron is never allowed loose: transferrin carries it, ferritin cages it, hepcidin locks the gates.
Deficiency
Iron deficiency -> microcytic anemia, fatigue, pica; most common nutritional deficiency worldwide (~25% of people).
Toxicity
Hemochromatosis -> cirrhosis, diabetes, cardiomyopathy. Acute overdose is lethal to children.
Sources
Heme iron (meat, best absorbed); legumes, spinach (boosted by vitamin C).
Co

Cobalt

Atomic number 27 · Ultratrace

Body store
~1 mg (as vitamin B12)
Where it lives
Exclusively as cobalamin (vitamin B12); liver stores 2-5 mg B12 - years of reserve.
Key figures
Daily need 2.4 ug B12. Serum B12 200-900 pg/mL.
Molecules
Methylcobalamin, adenosylcobalamin - organometallic Co-C bond, cycling Co3+/Co2+/Co+.
Reactions
Methionine synthase: homocysteine -> methionine (with folate - the methyl trap). Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (fatty acid metabolism). B12 deficiency -> demyelination + megaloblastic anemia.
Deficiency
Pernicious anemia, vegan diet without supplementation -> irreversible neuropathy if late.
Toxicity
Essentially none from B12 (water-soluble); cobalt metal toxicity is industrial, not nutritional.
Sources
Animal products only (meat, dairy, eggs); supplements/fortified foods for vegans.

Cu

Copper

Atomic number 29 · Trace

Body store
80-120 mg
Where it lives
Liver (main store), brain, heart, kidney; blood bound to ceruloplasmin (~90%).
Key figures
Serum 70-140 ug/dL; ceruloplasmin 20-50 mg/dL. Daily need 900 ug.
Molecules
Cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV); Cu/Zn-SOD; ceruloplasmin; lysyl oxidase; dopamine beta-hydroxylase; tyrosinase.
Reactions
Final electron handoff to O2 in respiration. Ceruloplasmin oxidizes Fe2+ -> Fe3+ so iron can load transferrin - copper is iron's logistics officer. Dopamine -> norepinephrine; tyrosine -> melanin (eye-color link).
Deficiency
Anemia unresponsive to iron, neutropenia, myelopathy, depigmentation.
Toxicity
Wilson disease (ATP7B mutation) -> copper in liver/brain/cornea (Kayser-Fleischer rings); acute -> hemolysis.
Sources
Shellfish, nuts, seeds, organ meats, whole grains, chocolate.
Zn

Zinc

Atomic number 30 · Trace

Body store
2-3 g
Where it lives
Muscle ~60%, bone ~30%, skin/liver/prostate/eye/immune cells; no dedicated store - needs steady intake.
Key figures
Serum 70-120 ug/dL. Daily need 11 mg (men) / 8 mg (women). ~10% of human proteins contain zinc fingers.
Molecules
Zinc fingers (DNA binding); carbonic anhydrase; DNA/RNA polymerases; Cu/Zn-SOD.
Reactions
Structural (zinc fingers grip DNA), catalytic (carbonic anhydrase: CO2 + H2O <-> HCO3- + H+, ~10^6/s), immune signaling. Zn2+/Cu2+ compete for the same transporter.
Deficiency
Dermatitis, alopecia, diarrhea, immune failure, growth retardation.
Toxicity
Excess Zn (>40 mg/day) blocks copper absorption -> copper-deficiency anemia; acute -> nausea.
Sources
Meat, shellfish (oysters richest), legumes, nuts, whole grains.

Se

Selenium

Atomic number 34 · Trace

Body store
~15 mg
Where it lives
Selenoproteins in liver, kidney, thyroid, muscle, immune cells.
Key figures
Serum 70-150 ug/L. Daily need 55 ug. 25 human selenoproteins.
Molecules
Selenocysteine (21st amino acid) in glutathione peroxidases, thioredoxin reductases, deiodinases.

Toxicity

Brazil nuts (extremely rich), seafood, meat, grains (soil-dependent).

Atomic number 42 · Ultratrace

Body store
~5 mg
Where it lives

Molybdopterin cofactor in xanthine oxidase, sulfite oxidase, aldehyde oxidase.
Reactions

The brain — central processing unit, liquid-cooled

The most power-dense tissue you own: 20 watts on 2% of body mass, cooled entirely by fluid.

Cooling loop 1 — cerebrospinal fluid

150 mL of crystal-clear coolant; the body manufactures ~500 mL daily, turning the whole volume over 3–4 times a day. Circuit: ventricles → aqueduct → subarachnoid space → venous blood. Chemistry is exact — Na⁺ 138–142, K⁺ 2.8–3.0, protein only 15–45 mg/dL. Any protein in the coolant means the filter is breached. It also cuts the brain's effective weight from 1,400 g to ~25–50 g, and flushes metabolic waste every night during deep sleep.

Cooling loop 2 — cerebral blood flow

Carotids + vertebrals feed the Circle of Willis — a redundant arterial ring with built-in rerouting. Active regions get more blood within seconds: dynamic cooling for hot cores. The blood–brain barrier blocks 98% of molecules from the coolant.

Thermal failure modes

  • Fever (38–40 °C): deliberate set-point raise — the system overheats itself to cook pathogens.
  • Hyperthermia (>40.5 °C): proteins denature, ion gradients collapse. The CPU has no thermal shutdown circuit.
  • Therapeutic hypothermia (32–34 °C): deliberate underclocking — buys time after cardiac arrest.
  • Status epilepticus: thermal runaway — demand triples, heat outruns both loops.

Eye color — why no two eyes match

There is no blue pigment in a blue eye. Only brown-black eumelanin (C, H, N, O) and red-yellow pheomelanin (plus sulfur) exist — and the copper enzyme tyrosinase builds both. Blue is light scattering off iris collagen: structural color, like the sky.

The reasoning

  • Two lotteries: genetics sets the palette (16+ genes, HERC2 the master dimmer — one common ancestor ~6,000–10,000 years ago for blue eyes).
  • Developmental chaos paints the pattern: crypts, furrows, and freckles form in the womb through tissue buckling — the same mathematics as fingerprints and snowflakes.
  • The twin test: identical twins share 100% of color genes, yet iris scanners tell them apart. If genes drew the pattern, twins would match. They don't.
  • Stable for life, ~240+ degrees of freedom — a biometric frozen in childhood.

What elements combine to build — and why

Every bond in your body is atoms solving one problem: filling their outer electron shell — by sharing (covalent: water, DNA), donating (ionic: salt, bone mineral), or handing off electrons one at a time (redox: iron and copper in respiration).

Transmutation — elements becoming other elements

Chemistry rearranges atoms. Nuclear change rewrites them.

In your body, right now

Potassium-40 decays to argon-40 and calcium-40 — about 4,000 atoms transmute inside you every second (half-life 1.25 billion years). Carbon-14 becomes nitrogen-14 (half-life 5,730 years). You are a low-level nuclear reactor that never switches off.

In hospitals

Fluorine-18 decays to oxygen-18 by positron emission (half-life ~110 min). The positrons annihilate into gamma rays — that flash is what a PET scanner images.

In stars

The Big Bang made hydrogen and helium. Stars fused H → He → C → N → O, up to iron. Supernovae forged everything heavier. Every atom in your body is a transmutation product — you are not just made of elements, you are made of elements becoming other elements.

Frontiers — the leading edge

What the newest science says about the chemistry, the CPU, and the eyes. Every finding below is published and replicated; dates mark when the field turned.

Biochemistry — new reactions, new rules

Ferroptosis (2012)

Iron-dependent cell death. When lipid peroxidation outruns repair, iron catalyzes a lethal chain reaction through cell membranes. The master brake is GPX4 — a selenoprotein: glutathione peroxidase 4, with selenocysteine at its catalytic center. Iron loads the gun, selenium holds the safety. Now a drug target in stroke, neurodegeneration, and cancer (tumors can be pushed into ferroptosis).

Fe · Se

Cuproptosis (2022)

Copper-dependent cell death. Excess copper binds lipoylated enzymes of the Krebs cycle, forcing them into toxic aggregates. A second metal-gated death program, distinct from ferroptosis — the cell has separate tripwires for separate metals. Copper ionophores are in cancer trials.

Cu

Cryo-EM revolution (2017 Nobel)

Flash-freezing biomolecules and imaging them with electrons now resolves membrane proteins, ribosomes mid-translation, and spliceosomes at near-atomic detail — structures that resisted crystallography for decades. Drug design moved from guessing to seeing.

methods

Designed proteins (2024 Nobel)

David Baker's lab designs entirely new proteins from scratch — enzymes and binders that never existed in nature. Demis Hassabis and John Jumper's AlphaFold predicts how proteins fold and dock. Biochemistry is becoming an engineering discipline.

methods

mRNA therapeutics (2023 Nobel)

Karikó and Weissman's modified nucleosides made synthetic mRNA invisible to innate immunity — the platform behind COVID vaccines, now in trials as personalized cancer vaccines. The body becomes its own drug factory, transiently.

N · P

Microbiome chemistry

~38 trillion gut microbes form a parallel chemical organ: they synthesize GABA, serotonin precursors, and short-chain fatty acids that feed colon cells and signal the brain. The gut–brain axis is molecular, not metaphorical.

C · H · N

Brain — mapping and mending the CPU

Glymphatic system (2012)

During deep sleep the brain's interstitial space expands ~60% and cerebrospinal fluid flushes through, clearing beta-amyloid and metabolic waste via aquaporin-4 channels. The nightly coolant flush is real, measured, and one reason sleep deprivation raises dementia risk.

H₂O · sleep

Human brain cell census (2023)

The BRAIN Initiative mapped 3,000+ distinct cell types across the human brain — the first complete parts list of the CPU. Each type has its own gene-expression and connectivity signature.

mapping

Microglia — the gardeners

The brain's resident immune cells prune synapses, sculpting circuits through childhood and clearing debris in aging. TREM2 variants that cripple microglia sharply raise Alzheimer's risk — the cleanup crew matters as much as the wiring.

immune

Anti-amyloid antibodies (2023–24)

Lecanemab and donanemab — antibodies that clear amyloid plaques — became the first FDA-approved disease-modifying Alzheimer's drugs. Benefit is modest and risks (brain swelling/bleeding) are real: a first foothold, not a cure.

therapy

Brain–computer interfaces (2024)

Neuralink's first human implant and Synchron's stentrode let paralyzed users move cursors and type by thought. The CPU's output bus can now be tapped directly.

interface

Adult neurogenesis

New neurons are born in the adult hippocampus throughout life, declining with age — confirmed in humans, though the exact rate is still debated. The CPU does limited self-repair.

plasticity

Eyes — restoring and reprogramming vision

Melanopsin / ipRGCs (1998–2002)

A third photoreceptor system: intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells sense ambient light for circadian entrainment, pupil reflex, and mood — no image formed. Your eyes set your body clock through a channel you never see with.

light · circadian

Luxturna (2017)

The first FDA-approved gene therapy for inherited blindness: a working RPE65 gene delivered to the retina restores the visual cycle in Leber congenital amaurosis. Blindness treated at its genetic source.

gene therapy

EDIT-101 (2020)

The first CRISPR editing performed inside a human body — delivered to the eye for CEP290 blindness. The eye's immune privilege and small size make it gene therapy's beachhead organ.

CRISPR

Optogenetic vision (2021)

Sahel and colleagues gave a blind retinitis pigmentosa patient an algae light-sensor protein (ChrimsonR) plus light-amplifying goggles — restoring partial, useful vision. Blind retinas reprogrammed to see with borrowed proteins.

optogenetics

PRIMA implant

A photovoltaic chip placed under the retina converts incoming light to electrical pulses, replacing lost photoreceptors in age-related macular degeneration — a solar panel for the eye.

neuroprosthetic

Myopia epidemic

Half the world is projected to be myopic by 2050. The leading mechanism: insufficient outdoor light → low retinal dopamine → excessive eye elongation. Bright light is a drug for the developing eye.

light · dopamine
