The Artist

Taylor Heller is an inventor and builder working at the intersection of engineering and visual art. Named inventor on eight patent applications spanning suspension systems, admission-control gates, and error-correction codes, he brings an engineer's discipline to AI-assisted image-making: every series is governed by a locked aesthetic system — composition rules, color hierarchies, and geometric constraints held constant across all variations.

Trains of Thought — his wire-tunnel masterpiece series — treats the train as a moving thought: chrome wire tunnels, striated iridescent walls, marbled floors, cities folded into rings and spinning against the clouds. Everything before this series, he says, was practice. Working iteratively with generative models, he art-directs each piece through dozens of generations, keeping only what obeys the lock.

His work has been built for the browser, the headset, and the gallery wall — always with the same method: define the lock, generate within it, keep only what survives.

The Story — a dialogue

Dreamers: the Conductor, the Traveler. Scene: a platform of combed light.

Conductor: Ticket.

Traveler: I haven't one. I have a memory of one.

Conductor: Then let us examine what you hold. Is a memory of a ticket a ticket?

Traveler: It is not. A ticket is punched, and admits you. A memory only describes the admitting.

Conductor: And yet you are standing on my platform, and the train has come for you. So tell me — which of the two admitted you: the paper, or the memory of the paper?

Traveler: I… came by remembering the way.

Conductor: Then a memory may do the work of the thing it remembers. Remember this conclusion; we will need it when the floor changes its mind.

Traveler: The floor?

Conductor: It was rose when you arrived. What is it now?

Traveler: Teal. It changed while I was answering you.

Conductor: And is it the same floor?

Traveler: It must be. I have not moved.

Conductor: Then a thing may change everything about itself and remain itself — provided someone stands on it and does not move. Hold still, then, in more ways than one. What do you see in the train's window?

Traveler: A small pink dusk. And inside the dusk, another train, arriving.

Conductor: And inside that one?

Traveler: I cannot see. It is too far and too near at once.

Conductor: Good. You have described every thought you have ever had. Now look down the platform, and tell me what stands at the last doorway.

Traveler: Nothing. An empty hall. No train, no light — the pause holds its breath.

Conductor: And is the nothing a station?

Traveler: If the train stops there, it is a station.

Conductor: The train stops there. Every journey on this line includes one stop where nothing happens — and the nothing, my friend, is load-bearing. Remove it, and the arrival arrives nowhere. Now, one warning, which I give as myth because the truth of it will not fit in an argument: at Ring City the world folds into a ring and spins, and thoughts fall out of open hands. So tell me before we depart — the thought you are riding, the one that brought you here by memory — are you holding it, or is it holding you?

Traveler: I don't know.

Conductor: Excellent. Then you may board. Nobody rides this train who thinks they know.
